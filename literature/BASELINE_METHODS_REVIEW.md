## Bottom line

The baseline the team has already built (a time-indexed QUBO with start-window pruning, a horizon search for makespan, QAOA on the 2×2 and a QUBO sampler on the 3×3) is the canonical approach in the literature. It traces directly to the D-Wave job-shop formulation of [Venturelli et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.1506.08479) and its gate-model port by a Poznań group, [Kurowski et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejor.2023.03.013). Points will therefore not come from the formulation family itself. The literature points to three places where a team can still differentiate: (i) a provably smaller correct encoding (domain-wall, position-based or compact job-shop encodings), (ii) an honest algorithm comparison with constraint-aware QAOA variants, and (iii) an extension where QUBO solving sits inside a hybrid decomposition (rescheduling after disruptions, machine unavailability, flexible routing), which is where VeloxQ's scale has a natural role. No published study in this sweep applies VeloxQ to manufacturing scheduling, so a careful one would be novel.

## Baseline formulation and makespan

### Time-indexed QUBOs are the standard

[Venturelli et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.1506.08479) formulated job-shop scheduling as a time-indexed QUBO, with one binary per (operation, start time), and treated makespan as a sequence of feasibility problems at decreasing horizons. Variable pruning and tailored binary searches over the horizon were central to fitting instances of up to six jobs and six machines on D-Wave hardware. This is exactly the structure of `solver/stage1.py`: penalty-only feasibility at horizon H, with an outer search on H. The organizer's reference counts (72 raw, 39 pruned variables at H=8) correspond to this formulation. [Shimada et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1109/iciea52957.2021.9436798) made the same decomposition explicit for Ising machines, alternating a makespan-estimation step with a constraint-satisfaction QUBO. [Carugno et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10169-0) is the most careful hardware evaluation of the time-indexed model. It stresses costs that are often ignored: building the QUBO, qubit overhead after embedding, and chain breaks. Those are the same items the hackathon's VeloxQ criterion asks teams to report.

### The same model on gate-based hardware

[Kurowski et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ejor.2023.03.013) (EJOR, the Poznań group of Kurowski, Różycki, Waligóra and Węglarz) built the QAOA cost Hamiltonian from the time-indexed representation and solved a basic JSSP instance. They found that optimal variational parameters form transferable patterns. Their earlier hybrid annealing heuristic, [Kurowski et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-50433-5_39), is the annealing counterpart. For Instance A these two papers are the closest published precedent and should be cited in the Project Design. [Amaro et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjqt/s40507-022-00123-4) is the best template for the organizer's suggested comparison of QAOA, VQE and annealing. On a steel-manufacturing JSSP run on IBM hardware, filtering VQE (F-VQE) converged faster and sampled the optimum more often than QAOA, standard VQE and VarQITE, and it scaled to 23 qubits without error mitigation. A recent non-variational alternative, [Lopez-Ruiz et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2510.26859) (preprint), combines fixed linear-ramp schedules with iterative warm starts on just-in-time JSSP instances on IonQ hardware. It is a useful contrast to parameter-optimized QAOA.

### Flow-shop-specific results

Both instances are flow shops, and the flow-shop quantum literature is thin. Most hits for "quantum flow shop" are classical quantum-inspired evolutionary algorithms, which are not relevant here. The relevant work is recent and conference-level. [Farhani et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3638530.3664150) compared a position-based permutation QUBO with TSP-style approximations on Taillard instances using D-Wave, and the position-based model won. [Farhani et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3712255.3734364) found that hand-crafted QUBOs beat automatically generated ones, and that results depend strongly on the solver. [Baioletti et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1145/3712255.3734312) avoided a Hamiltonian for makespan altogether: a variational circuit encodes the permutation in few qubits, and the objective is evaluated classically. [Fleury et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-62912-9_21) proposed an indirect rank-based coding for QAOA, and [Bożejko et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1109/cinti63048.2024.10830818) ran Qiskit flow-shop experiments on IBM hardware. [Saiem et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1145/3583133.3596378) explored Grover search. The classical anchor is [Johnson 1954](https://doi.org/10.1002/nav.3800010110). Its two-machine rule solves Instance A exactly (order Z2→Z1, makespan 4), and the team should cite it as the analytic reference for the 2×2.

A modelling point follows from this literature. Permutation encodings capture feasibility cheaply: for three jobs, nine one-hot position variables or fewer with domain walls. Permutation makespan is a max-plus recursion, however, and is not quadratic in those variables. Published work therefore either decodes classically (indirect approaches), approximates the objective, or returns to time indexing. A team claiming the "smallest correct QUBO" must state which of these it does.

## Smaller encodings and constraint handling

### Encodings

Encoding choice is the main lever for the organizer's "smallest correct QUBO" comparison. [Chancellor 2019](https://doi.org/10.1088/2058-9565/ab33c2) introduced domain-wall encoding: a k-valued variable uses k−1 qubits with only two-body ferromagnetic couplings, and two-body mixers can be built that stay in the logical subspace. [Chen et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1109/tqe.2021.3094280) found that domain-wall encoding outperformed one-hot on quantum annealers for every metric they tested. [Codognet 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/qce53715.2022.00036) extended domain walls to permutation constraints and nearly halved inter-qubit connections. The repo already reaches 30 domain-wall variables against 39 one-hot at H=8, consistent with this literature. A more aggressive option is [Schmid et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1088/2058-9565/ad9cba). Their job-shop encoding cuts the number of bit-strings by at least a factor N/log₂N relative to time-indexed encodings, and it improved variational performance. It also covers flexible job shops, which matters if Stage 2 adds alternative machines. [Sawaya et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.22331/q-2023-09-14-1111) provide an encoding-independent intermediate representation and numerical comparisons of one-hot, unary and binary encodings, including machine scheduling. It is the right reference for justifying an encoding choice rather than asserting it.

### Constraint-preserving QAOA

Penalty QAOA wastes amplitude on infeasible states. The repo's earlier shared-X 2×2 runs showed about 89–95% feasible samples. Subsequent local refinement selected independent-X p=3 QAOA, observing 40,960/40,960 feasible shots across ten new seeds on this same tiny noiseless instance; see `docs/STAGE1_REFINEMENT.md`. This does not establish a general feasibility guarantee. The quantum alternating operator ansatz of [Hadfield et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.3390/a12020034) restricts mixing to the feasible subspace. [Wang et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1103/physreva.101.012320) showed that XY mixers over one-hot variables can be implemented in depth O(k) and clearly beat the transverse-field mixer. [Fuchs et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/a15060202) generalised this to arbitrary feasible subspaces with cheaper CX counts. [Binkowski et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.20935/acadquant7900) derived feasibility-preserving mixers for open-shop scheduling from the problem's symmetry group, a close structural relative of the flow shop. Two low-cost add-ons are documented for small instances: CVaR aggregation of samples, which gave faster convergence in every problem tested by [Barkoutsos et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.22331/q-2020-04-20-256), and warm starts from a classical relaxation ([Egger et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.22331/q-2021-06-17-479)). [Pecyna & Różycki 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/app14188099), again from Poznań, report higher feasible-sample probabilities from problem-specific Hamiltonian construction than from standard penalty formulations.

### Penalty weights

All-unit penalty weights are valid for pure feasibility, which is the current repo design. Once an objective term or a soft constraint (tardiness, setup cost, churn) enters, weight selection becomes a correctness issue, not only a tuning one. [Ayodele 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-04148-8_11) studies penalty weights specifically for permutation QUBOs. [Verma & Lewis 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.disopt.2020.100594) give bounds and partitioning techniques. [Montañez-Barrera et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1088/2058-9565/ad35e4) encode inequality constraints without slack variables through unbalanced penalization, directly applicable to due-date or capacity inequalities. [Phillipson & Hutten 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cor.2026.107667) show for parallel-machine makespan that a compact load-balancing QUBO stays within a few percent of the lower bound, while penalty-sensitive formulations degrade with size.

## Extensions and the role of VeloxQ

### Extensions with published precedent

Each candidate Stage-2 direction in the repo's ideation ledger has at least one QUBO precedent to build on and compare against. For **disruptions and rescheduling**, Schworm and co-workers progressed from static flexible job shops ([Schworm et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11740-022-01145-8)) to multi-objective FJSSP with makespan, workload and priority via decomposition and bottleneck-driven subproblem selection ([Schworm et al. 2023a](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmsy.2023.11.015)). They then added dynamic scheduling under sudden disturbances ([Schworm et al. 2023b](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.procir.2023.09.028)) and rescheduling with an explicit rescheduling-effort objective alongside makespan ([Schworm et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mfglet.2024.09.066)). Their 2026 parameter study ([Schworm et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dte.2026.100132)) found that the operations-per-iteration count dominated solution variance and recommended subproblems of at most about 30 operations. This is useful evidence for sizing a residual QUBO. For **machine outages**, [Aggoune & Deleplanque 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-62912-9_23) and [Deleplanque et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10732-026-09594-5) treat JSSP with availability constraints by quantum annealing. **AGV transport**, central to the repo's "MetalTech under pressure" design, appears in [Geitz et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-08011-1_10), which compares classical and quantum approaches. [Pérez Armas et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-67168-6) compared twelve MILP formulations of resource-constrained project scheduling to pick the most qubit-efficient one before converting it to a QUBO. That is a methodology the organizer's "report variable cost per added constraint" requirement essentially asks for. Polish railway rescheduling ([Domino et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11128-022-03670-y); [Domino et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/e25020191)) offers a disruption-recovery QUBO and HOBO template with classical ILP and tensor-network references solved on real network data.

### Hybrid decomposition

The strongest methodological signal in this literature is that QUBO solvers do best inside a classical search, not as stand-alone schedulers. [Zhang et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1609/icaps.v32i1.19826) (ICAPS) found that direct JSP QUBOs were not competitive. Their large-neighbourhood search, which uses a QUBO solver for the neighbourhood and constraint programming for the rest, outperformed CP-based LNS and MIP and was competitive with CP on large instances. [Tran et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1609/socs.v7i1.18390) used annealer samples to prune and order nodes in a complete classical tree search. [Denkena et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cirpj.2021.03.006) integrated quantum-based flexible job-shop optimisation into running production-control IT. The repo's "closed recovery neighbourhood" proposal (freeze history, solve a residual QUBO) matches this pattern, and it gives a principled answer to the organizer's demand for a CP-SAT reference on the same model.

### VeloxQ: what is known

[Pawłowski et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2501.19221) (preprint, with co-authors from Wrocław University of Science and Technology, the Polish Academy of Sciences and Quantumz.io) describe VeloxQ as a classical QUBO solver for conventional hardware. They benchmark it against D-Wave Advantage and Advantage2, Kipu's BF-DCQO, simulated bifurcation, tensor networks, CPLEX and branch-and-bound, and report competitive or superior quality and runtime across regimes. It was the only solver they could run on the largest sparse instances, with up to about 10⁸ variables. Independent use in a scheduling-type problem is still rare. [Kędziera et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2512.19340) (preprint) applied VeloxQ to rolling-stock circulation for Silesian Railways and found it outperformed D-Wave on small instances. At scale, though, the limit was generating and storing the QUBO, not solving it. [Hanussek et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1103/rrf3-jm5m) describe VeloxQ as a GPU-accelerated, dynamical-systems-based heuristic that returns a low-energy spectrum of solutions. VeloxQ should therefore be presented honestly as quantum-inspired classical hardware, not as a quantum run. Instance A's quantum requirement must stay with QAOA or another genuine quantum algorithm. The literature suggests VeloxQ is most compelling in three settings. The first is many large, sparse residual QUBOs solved repeatedly, as in rescheduling or LNS. The second is scaling sweeps where embedding-bound annealers and statevector QAOA cannot follow. The third is comparisons of encodings (one-hot, domain-wall, compact) on identical instances, with time-to-target and feasibility rate as metrics.

## Gaps to exploit

1. No paper in this sweep reports VeloxQ on machine scheduling. A reproducible encoding × solver × size study on flow and job shops would be new.
2. Flow-shop QUBO work is limited to conference papers on D-Wave or simulators. A rigorous smallest-correct-encoding argument for permutation flow shops, including how makespan is recovered, is not settled in the literature.
3. Published quantum rescheduling studies rarely report the restriction gap between the residual neighbourhood and the full re-optimisation, or a CP-SAT reference on the same model. The [Werner & Ullinger 2026](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjqt/s40507-026-00494-y) survey explicitly criticises the comparability of results across this literature.

For framing and for the jury's likely questions on quantum advantage: [Abbas et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42254-024-00770-9) on challenges and opportunities in quantum optimization; [Yarkoni et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1088/1361-6633/ac8c54) on quantum annealing in industry; [Lucas 2014](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphy.2014.00005) and [Glover et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10288-019-00424-y) for QUBO and Ising formulation basics; and [Werner & Ullinger 2026](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjqt/s40507-026-00494-y) for a 20-year survey of quantum scheduling algorithms.
