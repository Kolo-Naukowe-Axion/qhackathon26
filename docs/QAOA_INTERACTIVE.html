QAOA, built from arrows

An interactive guide for the qhackathon26 scheduling baseline. It picks up where sections 1–5 of the methods tutorial stop: you know what bits, the energy E(x)=x⊤Qx+c and the encodings are. Every picture on this page is computed live in your browser by an exact simulation of the quantum state. Move the sliders, press the buttons.

1The question QAOA answers

We use the smallest scheduling problem that still has a clash: one machine, two jobs A and B, each 1 hour, deadline H=2. Each job may start at hour 0 or hour 1, so one bit per job is enough: yA=1 means "A starts at hour 1", yA=0 means "A starts at hour 0" (same for B).

E(yA,yB)=1−yA−yB+2yAyB
x=yAyBA startsB startsE(x)meaning
00001clash at hour 0
01010A then B ✓
10100B then A ✓
11111clash at hour 1
The goal. Find a bitstring with the lowest energy. If that energy is 0, a schedule fitting in H hours exists, and the bitstring is that schedule. To find the shortest schedule we later repeat this with smaller H (section 11).

Of course here we can read the answer off the table. The point is that for n bits the table has 2n rows (Instance B: 230≈109), and we want a method that never writes the table down.

2A quantum state is a list of arrows

A classical computer holds one bitstring at a time. Two qubits hold a list of four numbers, one per bitstring:

state=(c00, c01, c10, c11)

Each cx is a complex number. Draw it as an arrow in a circle: the arrow's length is |cx| and its direction is the complex phase. Two rules are all you need:

Rule 1 (measurement). Measuring all qubits gives one bitstring x, with probability P(x)=|cx|2, the arrow's length squared. The list itself is never seen; only that one bitstring comes out.
Rule 2 (gates). We cannot write the list directly. A gate is an operation on one or two qubits that turns the list into a new list. Lengths squared always add up to 1.
Try it · direction does not matter for probability

Drag the arrow (or click anywhere in the circle). The direction changes, the length stays 1/2, so the probability stays 12.

3The start: equal arrows = random guessing

A Hadamard gate on each qubit, applied to |00⟩, gives every bitstring the same arrow:

(c00, c01, c10, c11)=(12, 12, 12, 12)

It does not depend on the problem at all: we do not know which bitstrings are good, so we start fair. Measured, it gives a valid schedule with probability V/2n where V is the number of valid bitstrings: here 2/4=12. That is exactly random guessing. For Instance A at H=4 it is only 1/16.

Try it · measure the start state QPU

Everything QAOA does from here on is a way to push this fraction up, using only the energy E.

4The cost step: rotate each arrow by its energy

The cost step multiplies every amplitude by a phase that depends on its energy:

cx⟶e−iγE(x)cx

A phase e−iθ is a complex number of length 1, so multiplying by it rotates the arrow by −θ and keeps its length. With E=0 the arrow does not move; with E=1 it turns by −γ. This is the only place where the problem enters the circuit.

Try it · the cost step QPU
Look at the probability bars: they never move. Rotating an arrow does not change its length, so after the cost step alone the probability of a valid schedule is still V/2n. The energy is now written into the arrows' directions, but invisibly. We need a step that turns directions into lengths.

5The mixer gate on one qubit

Start with one qubit, so there are only two arrows, (c0,c1). A one-qubit gate is a 2×2 matrix, and applying it means multiplying this matrix by the column of amplitudes. The two simplest gates are "do nothing" and "flip the bit":

I=[1001],X=[0110],X[c0c1]=[c1c0]

We want a dial between them, with knob β: at β=0 nothing happens, at β=π/2 the bit is flipped, in between part of each arrow moves to the other bitstring. The mixer is that blend:

M(β)=cosβI−isinβX=[cosβ−isinβ−isinβcosβ]

Multiplying out the matrix–vector product row by row:

c0new=cosβc0−isinβc1
c1new=−isinβc0+cosβc1

Each new arrow is a kept part (its own old arrow times cosβ) plus a received part (the other arrow times −isinβ). On hardware this gate is called RX(2β).

Why cos, sin and the −i? So that probabilities still add up to 1 for every β and every starting state. Tick "remove the −i" below with the equal start (1/2,1/2) and β=π/4: the total becomes 2, which is impossible. The −i makes M(β) a legal quantum gate. (It also equals e−iβX, but you do not need that here.)

Try it · the mixer as a dial

From (1,0) you get (cosβ, −isinβ), so P(0)=cos2β and P(1)=sin2β. From the equal start the probabilities stay 12 for every β: watch the arrows turn but the bars stay put.

6Interference: why the mixer needs the cost step

This is the one genuinely new idea in QAOA. Toy problem with one qubit: 0 is good (E=0), 1 is bad (E=1). The mixer builds each new arrow by adding two arrows head to tail: the kept part and the received part.

the two parts arriving at a bitstringtheir sumprobability
same directionlong arrowlarge
opposite directionsnothingzero
at right anglesin betweenin between
Try it · kept part + received part
  • Untick the cost step. The parts arriving at both bitstrings are always at right angles, so both sums have the same length: still a coin flip, for every β. The mixer alone cannot tell good from bad, because it does not know E.
  • With the cost step the bad arrow was turned by −π/2. At β=−π/4 the two parts line up at 0 and cancel at 1: P(0)=1. At β=+π/4 it is the other way round: everything lands on the bad bitstring.
The whole mechanism. The cost step marks bitstrings by turning their arrows according to energy (invisible). The mixer adds neighbouring arrows, which turns the marks into probability (visible). Whether probability piles up on good or on bad bitstrings depends on the angles γ,β, and that is why they have to be tuned.

7One full QAOA layer on two qubits

Back to the two-job problem: four arrows. One layer is: start (H on each qubit) → cost step with γ → mixer M(β) on qubit A and on qubit B → measure. Applying the one-qubit mixer to both qubits means every new arrow collects a contribution from all four old arrows, weighted by how many bits differ:

how y differs from xweight w(x,y)
same bitstringcos2β
neighbour (1 bit differs)−isinβcosβ
2 bits differ(−isinβ)2=−sin2β
cxnew=∑yw(x,y)cy
Try it · one layer, and the landscape of all angle pairs

The map on the right is the landscape: for every pair (γ,β) it shows the average energy you would measure after one layer. Dark is good. At (γ,β)=(π/2, 3π/8) the clashes cancel completely and every shot is a valid schedule; at (π/2, π/8) the good schedules cancel instead. Along γ=0 the map is flat at ⟨E⟩=0.5: no cost step, no progress.

The catch: on a real problem we cannot draw this map. Each pixel needs the full list of arrows, which exists only inside the quantum chip. All we can get out is measured bitstrings. Section 10 is how we search the map anyway.

8Building the cost step from gates (no 2n energies)

A natural worry: to apply e−iγE(x) to every bitstring, don't we have to compute E(x) for all 2n of them? No. The cost step is built from the terms of E, not from its values. Because a phase of a sum is a product of phases, ea+b=eaeb:

e−iγE=e−iγ·e+iγyA·e+iγyB·e−2iγyAyB
factorone gatewhich arrows it turns
e+iγyARZ on qubit Aevery bitstring with yA=1, by +γ
e+iγyBRZ on qubit Bevery bitstring with yB=1, by +γ
e−2iγyAyBRZZ on qubits A, Bevery bitstring with yA=yB=1, by −2γ
e−iγnoneall arrows equally, so nothing measurable changes: dropped

A gate on qubit A does not visit the arrows one by one: it is one physical operation on one qubit, and its effect on the bookkeeping list is that all bitstrings with yA=1 turn at once.

Try it · apply the gates one by one (γ=π/4)

After gates 1–3 every arrow has turned by −γE(x)+γ, the right phase up to one common turn; gate 4 (the constant) only adds that common turn.

How the cost grows

A QUBO has at most one linear term per bit and one pair term per pair of bits, so one cost step needs at most n+n(n−1)/2=n(n+1)/2 gates: about n2/2, never 2n. The ordinary computer does evaluate E(x), but only for the bitstrings that come back as shots: about a thousand per try.

9Which gates, and are they arbitrary?

partgatesfixed or chosen?
startH on every qubitchosen to match the mixer: fair to all bitstrings, and the X mixer alone leaves it unchanged
costRZ (one-bit terms), RZZ (two-bit terms)fixed by Q; only γ is free
mixerRX(2β) on every qubit (standard), or XY gates on pairsa design choice, with hard rules

Rules for any mixer. (1) It must move amplitude between bitstrings (add arrows of different bitstrings); a gate that only turns arrows would be another cost step. (2) It must be able to reach every bitstring that could be the answer. A QUBO cost needs only RZ and RZZ; a higher-order (HUBO) term like y1y2y3 would need a three-qubit phase gate, which is one reason we reduce everything to a QUBO first.

Lines show which arrows a mixer adds together. The XY mixer only mixes 01 ↔ 10 and never changes how many 1s there are. With the one-hot encoding ("exactly one start time per job") that keeps every arrow inside the valid patterns.

The choice matters. In an earlier exact simulation of Instance A at p=3, run with the same optimiser budget for all three set-ups, the probability of the optimal schedule was:

encoding + mixerqubitsP(optimal)
one-hot + X mixer80.11
one-hot + XY mixer80.89
domain wall + X mixer40.89

Why this recipe at all? QAOA is a step-by-step version of quantum annealing: start in the start state (the lowest-energy state of the mixer) and slowly change from "mixer only" to "cost only". If that is done slowly enough, the system ends in the lowest-energy state of the cost, i.e. the best schedule. Alternating cost and mixer layers imitates that slow change in chunks (Farhi, Goldstone, Gutmann 2014). With enough layers QAOA can reach the optimum; with few layers the tuned angles do the best they can. On real hardware a compiler rewrites each gate into the chip's native gates (e.g. RZZ becomes CNOT, RZ, CNOT); the effect on the arrows is identical.

10The QAOA loop

We cannot see the arrows or draw the landscape; we can only run the circuit and collect bitstrings. So we score a pair of angles by what comes out, and let an ordinary optimiser improve the score.

stepwherewhat happens
0CPUwrite the problem as E(x)=x⊤Qx+c
1CPUturn each term of E into a gate (RZ, RZZ); the mixer is RX(2β) on every qubit
2CPUchoose the depth p (layers) and starting angles: 2p numbers
3QPUrun the circuit: H on all qubits, then (cost, mixer) ×p, then measure. Repeat ~1000 times (shots). Only bitstrings come back
4CPUscore: E of each returned bitstring, averaged
5CPUthe optimiser proposes new angles that should lower the score; back to step 3; stop when the score stops improving
6CPUanswer: the lowest-energy bitstring among the final shots, decoded and checked
score(γ,β)=1S∑s=1SE(x(s))≈⟨E⟩
Try it · the loop on the two-job problem

The optimiser here is Nelder–Mead, a simple derivative-free method of the same family as COBYLA (used in the repository). It sees only the noisy shot averages, never the map. Click the map before running to start somewhere else.

11Reading off the answer, and the shortest schedule

The average energy is used only for tuning. The answer is one bitstring: among the shots from the final angles, take the one with the lowest E, decode it into start times and check it with an ordinary schedule validator. One measurement gives a whole bitstring (every qubit measured), not a single number.

One run answers a yes/no question: "is there a schedule that fits in H hours?" The horizon search repeats the whole loop with smaller H (fewer bits, new E). The smallest H that still yields an E=0 bitstring is the optimal makespan. For Instance A, H=3 is impossible: the lathe has 3 hours of work in total, so whichever job is turned second finishes on the lathe at hour 3 at the earliest and still needs the mill. The optimum is H=4.

QAOA is a heuristic: if it never returns an E=0 bitstring, that does not prove none exists. That is why the tiny instances are also checked by brute force.

12The real baseline: Instance A on 4 qubits

Instance A (jobs Z1 and Z2, route lathe T → mill F) at H=4 in the domain-wall encoding has four bits. Each bit says "this operation starts at its later allowed hour":

bit= 0= 1
y1Z1 lathe at 0Z1 lathe at 1
y2Z1 mill at 2Z1 mill at 3
y3Z2 lathe at 0Z2 lathe at 1
y4Z2 mill at 1Z2 mill at 2
E=2−y2+y3−y1y2+y1y3+y2y4−y3y4
Q=[0−1100−101001−10000],c=2

Only 1100 has E=0: Z2 lathe 0–1, Z1 lathe 1–3, Z2 mill 1–3, Z1 mill 3–4, makespan 4. Two linear terms give two RZ gates, four pair terms give four RZZ gates. Each angle is 2γ× its Ising coefficient (h2=0.5, h3=−0.5, J12=J34=−0.25, J13=J24=0.25, from y=(1−z)/2).

Try it · Instance A, p = 3 (16 arrows)

Click any bitstring to see the schedule it encodes. "Tuned angles" are the best of several optimiser restarts (exact simulation); "run optimiser" does one run from a ramp start (γ increasing, |β| decreasing, like a slow anneal) with 512 shots per try.

With the tuned angles, P(1100) grows layer by layer from 1/16≈0.06 to 0.18, 0.51 and finally 0.96 (each cost step leaves it unchanged; each mixer raises it). Instance B needs 30 qubits in this encoding: 230 arrows, still about 465 cost gates at most per layer.

13What is real on this page, and what is not

  • Every arrow, probability and shot here comes from an exact state-vector simulation running in your browser, with seeded pseudo-random shots. None of it ran on quantum hardware; ODRA 5 has not been used yet.
  • The repository's own runs use the Qiskit Aer simulator and optimise the angles on exact probabilities. The team's selected Stage 1 result (output/stage1-selected.json) uses a different, compact order QUBO, not the time-indexed domain-wall model shown here.
  • VeloxQ is a classical (quantum-inspired) solver, not a quantum computer.
Check yourself (answers hidden)

1. After the cost step alone, what is P(valid)? Still V/2n: phases have length 1.

2. With γ=0, can any β help? No: without marks, the mixer treats all bitstrings alike (flat line on the map).

3. How many gates does one cost step need for n bits? At most n(n+1)/2, one per non-zero entry of Q.

4. What leaves the chip? Only bitstrings. Energies, averages and new angles are all computed on the CPU.
